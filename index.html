About

I am a pediatric nephrologist at Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, Linkou, and a PhD student in the Division of Artificial Intelligence, Graduate Institute of Clinical Medical Sciences, Chang Gung University.

My clinical work centers on children with kidney disease and electrolyte disorders. My research asks a narrower question: can routinely collected clinical signals — an electrocardiogram, an ultrasound image, a sequence of bedside laboratory values — be read by a model to surface information a clinician cannot see directly, early enough to change a decision?

Research interests: AI-augmented medical education · inherited kidney diseases · acid-base and electrolyte disorders · interventional nephrology · pediatric critical care nephrology · evidence-based medicine.

I care most about the part that comes after a good AUC: whether a model is calibrated, whether it holds up outside the hospital that built it, and whether anyone's care actually improves because it exists.

Research

Lead project

AI-enabled electrocardiography for monitoring serum potassium dynamics in severe hypokalemia

First author · American Journal of Kidney Diseases · Articles in Press, August 2026 · read the article

The problemSevere hypokalemia is treated by giving potassium and then checking it again. Each check means another blood draw and another wait. Potassium can also overshoot: correct too fast and the patient rebounds into hyperkalemia. The gap between blood tests is where that happens, unseen.
What was already knownA deep learning model can read a 12-lead ECG and estimate serum potassium at a single moment. Whether it could track potassium as it moves during treatment had not been tested.
What we didA multicenter retrospective cohort at three teaching hospitals in Taiwan: 191 adults with severe hypokalemia (serum K⁺ ≤ 2.5 mmol/L) and 466 paired ECG and laboratory measurements taken during potassium supplementation. Agreement was analyzed with linear mixed-effects models and repeated-measures correlation, not simple pooled correlation, because measurements repeat within the same patient.
My roleI co-originated the research question and study design, contributed to analysis and interpretation, and wrote the manuscript as first author.
0.847within-patient correlation between ECG-estimated and laboratory potassium (95% CI 0.81–0.88)
52.5 minaverage lead time of the ECG estimate over the laboratory result
0.920AUC for detecting K⁺ < 3.5 mmol/L (95% CI 0.863–0.961)
The finding that mattered mostAccuracy depended on why the potassium was low. When hypokalemia came from a sudden shift of potassium into cells, the ECG estimate tracked the laboratory value almost one-to-one (AUC 0.952). When it came from a chronic whole-body deficit, the signal was weaker and flatter (AUC 0.870). The same model is not equally trustworthy in two patients with the same number on the chart.
Why that is usefulPatients with an acute shift corrected about three times faster (0.121 vs 0.039 mmol/L per hour) — the group that moves fastest is the group the ECG reads best. In two patients with thyrotoxic periodic paralysis, the ECG flagged rebound hyperkalemia roughly 42 minutes before the laboratory confirmed it.
What it does not showThis was retrospective, mostly from one center, and in an Asian population. It did not test whether patient outcomes improve. The ECG estimate is a monitoring adjunct between blood draws, not a replacement for them.

What this is. A synthetic waveform, not patient data, drawn from the classical ECG changes of potassium disturbance: the T wave flattens then inverts as potassium falls, a U wave emerges, the ST segment sags; at high potassium the T wave becomes tall and narrow and the QRS widens.
What this is not. The model in our study (ECG12Net, 82 convolutional layers) reads the raw 12-lead waveform. It does not measure T wave height or U waves. This demo shows the physiology a clinician can see — part of why the model works, not how it works.
